Transmission of Light
Light is an electromagnetic wave. It can pass through a
material medium and a vacuum. The amount of light passing
through a body depends on the nature of the body.
If a large percentage of light falling on a body passes through it,
the body is said to be transparent, e.g. water, glass etc.
Bodies which allow some small amount of light to pass through
them are known as translucent, e.g. tissue, paper, e.t.c.
Other bodies that do not allow any light to pass through them
are called Opaque, e.g. wood, bricks, walls e.t.c.
Rays and Beams of Light
A light ray is the direction or path along which light energy
flows. A collection of rays is called a beam.
A light ray
Beam of light are of three types:
1. Parallel beam: When light rays are parallel to one another.
Example: Beam coming from the sun and car head lamp,
2. Convergent beam: This is when rays of light converge or
meet at a point. Example: Beam collected behind hand lens.
3. Divergent beam: This is when rays of light spread out or
diverge from a source or point. Example: Beam from an electric
bulb

1. Eclipse: This is a shadow cast by one heavenly body on

a. Eclipse of the sun or Solar Eclipse: The sun is a
luminous body while the moon and the earth are non
luminous. When the moon is between the sun and the
earth, the light from the sun will not reach the earth. The
shadow of the moon will be cast on the earth’s surface.
For people living in the umbra region, it will be total
eclipse (total darkness i.e. the light from the sun will be
completely blocked by the moon). Only people living in
the centre of the moon’s shadow can see, thus people

living in the penumbra region will experience partial
eclipse (i.e. partial darkness). For total eclipse to occur,
the sun, moon and earth must be in a direct line while
partial eclipse, the sun, the moon and the earth are not
exactly lined up.

Images Formed by Plane Mirror
A plane mirror is produced by coating one side of a plane

glass plate with silver. The silvered surface prevents light
rays that strike the glass from passing through the glass
and serves to reflect the light.
Formation of an image is one of the effects of reflection
of light. Image produced by a plane mirror is always virtual
(as a result of apparent intersection of rays that are
produced backwards by broken lines).
Properties of images formed by a plane mirror
The image formed by a plane mirror is always;
1. at the same distance behind the mirror as the object is
in front of the mirror.
2. of the same size as the size of the object
3. laterally inverted
4. virtual and erect.

Lateral inversion
The effect of the plane mirrors on objects placed in front
of it whereby the appearance of the image looks like a
reversal of the object is known as LATERAL INVERSION.
This is due to the fact that an object is perpendicularly
opposite to its image behind the mirror.

Principle of reversibility of light: This states that the path
of a light ray is reversible.

When two mirrors are inclined at an angle (θ) to each

A curved mirror or spherical mirror is a portion of a curved or
spherical surface, capable of reflecting light. It is made by

Types of Curved Mirrors
There are two types of curved mirrors;
1. Concave/Converging Mirrors: This is when the outside or
bulging surface of the mirror is silvered and the inside
surface is the reflecting part.
2. Convex/Diverging Mirrors: This is when the inside surface
of the mirror is silvered and the outside or bulging surface
is the reflecting part.

Disadvantages of convex mirror as driving mirror
1. The image is always smaller than the object
2. It gives a false impression of the distance as the image
seems further away, Hence the inscription on the mirror
“objects in the mirror are closer than they appear”.
Essential Parts of Spherical Mirrors
1. The Aperture: This is the width of the mirror (AB).
2. The Pole (P): This is the midpoint (center) of the reflecting
surface of the mirror
3. The Centre of Curvature (C): This is the center of the
hollow sphere of which the mirror forms a part.
4. The Radius of Curvature (r): This is the radius of the

sphere of which the mirror forms a part. The distance CP
(i.e. from the pole to the centre of curvature).
5. The Principal Axis (PC): This is the straight line joining the
pole and the centre of curvature.
6. The Principal Focus (F): This is the point on the principal
axis to which incident rays parallel and close to the
principal axis converge or diverge after reflection. Note:
The principal focus of a concave mirror is real focus
because the reflected rays actually pass through it. The
principal focus of a convex mirror is virtual focus because
the reflected rays do not pass through it.
7. Focal length (f): This is the distance between the pole and
the principal focus (FP). Note: f=r/2 (i.e. focal length =
radius of curvature/2).

LASER
“LASER” stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission
of Radiation. LASER is a device that generates an intense beam
of coherent monochromatic light.
Characteristics of Laser
1. It is highly coherent: The LASER beams are in phase with
each other over a sufficient time so that interference
effects can be visualized.
2. It is highly Monochromatic: The LASER beams typically
comes from one atomic transition via stimulated emission
and hence consist of single wavelength only.
3. It is highly collimated: LASER beams are very narrow,
sharp and highly amplified because of multiple reflections
caused by mirrored ends of laser cavity.
4. It is highly directional: A LASER beam spreads very little.
Types of LASERS
LASERS are classified into four types based on the type of laser
medium used;
1. Solid-state laser: This is a laser that uses solid as a laser
medium. In these lasers, glass or crystalline materials are
used. A ruby laser is a solid-state laser that uses the
synthetic ruby crystal as its laser medium. Light energy is
used as pump source.
2. Gas laser: This is a laser in which an electric current is
discharged through a gas inside the laser medium to
produce laser light.
3. Liquid laser: This is a laser that uses the liquid as laser
medium. Dye laser is an example that uses liquid solution

(dye) as the laser medium.
4. Semiconductor laser: This is also known as lasers diodes.
In semiconductor lasers, electrical energy is used as the
pump source. A p-n junction of a semiconductor is diode
forms the laser medium.
Applications of LASERS
LASER light is different from an ordinary light because it has
unique properties such as coherence, monochromacity,
directionality and high intensity.
Some applications of LASERS are;
1. In Medicine: i. They are used for bloodless surgery. ii.
They are used to destroy kidney stones. iii. They are used
in cancer diagnosis and therapy. iv. They are used for eye
lens curvature corrections v. They are used in fibre-optic
endoscope to detect ulcers in the intestines.
2. In Communications: i. Laser Light is used in optical fibre
communications to send information over large distances
with low loss. ii. Laser light is used in underwater
communication networks. iii. They are used in space
communication, radars and satellites.
3. In Industries: i. Lasers are used to cut glass and quartz. ii.
They are used for heat treatment in the automotive
industry. iii. They are used in electronic industries for
trimming the components of integrated circuits (ICs).
4. In Science and Technology: i. A LASER helps in studying
the Brownian motion of particles. ii. With the help of a
helium-neon laser, it was proved that the velocity of light is
the same in all directions. iii. They are used to count the
number of atoms in a substance.
5. In Military: i. Laser range finders are used to determine the
distance of an object. ii. Lasers are used to dispose the
energy of a warhead by damaging the missile. iii. Laser
Light is used in LIDAR’s to accurately measure the
distance to an object.

Terms Used in Refraction through Lenses
i.Centre of Curvature (C): This is the centres of the two
imaginary spheres of which the lens is a part. A lens has
two centre of curvature.
ii. Principal Axis: This is the imaginary line joining the
centres of curvatures of two spherical surfaces.

iii. Principal Focus (F): This is the point at which rays
closer and parallel to the principal axis diverge or
converge after refraction.
a. First Principal Focus (F ): It is a point on the
1
principal axis of lens, the rays starting from or
directed to which, become parallel to the principal
axis after refraction.
b. Second Principal Focus (F ): It is a point on the
2
principal axis at which the rays coming parallel to
the principal axis, converge on the other side of the
lens (convex) or appear to meet on the same side of
lens (concave), after refraction from the lens.
iv.Optical Centre (O): This is the point in the lens through
which rays of light passes undeviated.
v. Focal Length (f): This is the distance between the
principal focus and the optical centre.
vi. Focal Plane: This is an imaginary plane perpendicular to
the principal axis and passing through the focus of a lens.
vii.Aperture: This is the effective diameter of the circular
outline of a spherical lens.

Magnification (M) = image distance (v)/object distance (u) =
image height (H)/object height (h).
When the object distance (u) is known and the image distance
(v) is also known, the focal length (f) is calculated using 1/f =
1/u + 1/v, where f =r/2.
Power of Lens (p)
This is the reciprocal of the focal length. It is measured in
dioptre (dp) when the focal length is in metre (m). P= 1/f.
Note:
1. The thicker the lens , the more the power and the less the
focal length
2. For Concave or Diverging lens, f and v are Negative (i.e.
Virtual image)
3. For convex or Converging lens, f, and v are all Positive (i.e.
real image), v = -ve for virtual image or erect or upright or
image formed behind the lens.
